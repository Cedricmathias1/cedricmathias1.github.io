Overview

The problem worth solving.

Students at UCC juggle Canvas, Gmail, WhatsApp, Teams, and Moodle just to stay on top of one degree. The fragmentation is not an inconvenience it is actively causing missed deadlines, increased anxiety, and cognitive fatigue that gets in the way of learning.

The problem hits hardest for international students navigating a second language and students balancing part-time work. But it affects almost everyone with a full course load.

"How might we reduce cognitive load and help students manage academic communication more effectively across multiple fragmented platforms?"

USCH Dashboard unified view with priority actions, upcoming events, and important messages

The Problem

Three pain points, one root cause.

Initial research showed the fragmentation was not just inconvenient it was causing real anxiety and affecting academic performance.

01

Platform Fragmentation

Messages scattered across Canvas, Gmail, Teams, and WhatsApp. No single source of truth for anything academic.

02

Notification Overload

Duplicate notifications and constant interruptions causing stress and cognitive fatigue throughout the day.

03

Missed Information

Important deadlines buried in noise. Directly affecting academic performance and increasing anxiety.

Research

Eight interviews, four findings.

I ran eight semi-structured interviews with UCC students from diverse backgrounds international students, working students, and mature students. The goal was to understand communication behaviour at depth, not confirm assumptions.

01

Communication Fatigue

Students check 4–6 different platforms daily. An average of 45 minutes spent managing notifications before any actual academic work.

02

Anxiety and FOMO

Fear of missing information leads to compulsive platform-checking and heightened stress, especially around deadlines.

03

No Prioritisation System

No clear way to distinguish urgent academic messages from general social notifications across any existing platform.

04

Redundant Messages

Same announcements appearing across multiple platforms causing confusion about which version is authoritative.

Reflection

What I would do differently.

Run Diary Studies, Not Just Interviews

Interviews captured stated pain but not lived behaviour. Diary studies over a week would have caught moments of actual overload in real time far more valuable design data than retrospective recall.

Test Earlier with Lower Fidelity

I moved to mid-fi before testing the lo-fi properly. Navigation decisions challenged in round 2 could have been caught earlier and cheaper with paper prototype testing.

Include Academic Staff as a Persona

The system only works if lecturers actually use it to send announcements. Designing entirely from the student side left important constraints about the sending end unexplored.

Define Success Metrics Before Building

I measured overload reduction and ease of use in testing, but did not define what success looked like before starting design. Pre-set benchmarks would have made the evaluation cleaner and more defensible.

Key Learnings

What the project taught me.

Clarity Beats Features

Users preferred clean and minimal over feature-heavy every time. Visual hierarchy and clear categorisation reduced cognitive load more effectively than adding more controls ever could.

Research Validates Every Decision

Every significant design choice had a direct line back to an interview insight. The ones that did not were the ones challenged in testing. That correlation was not a coincidence.

Integration Is an Architecture Problem

The biggest UX problems were rooted in data architecture decisions. Building a unified inbox means thinking about how different systems talk to each other from day one not as an afterthought.

Anxiety Comes From Caring Too Much

The students who struggled most were not the least organised they were the most committed. Overload is a symptom of engagement, not laziness. That reframe shaped every design decision I made.
