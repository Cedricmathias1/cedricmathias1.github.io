The Brief

A product problem that turned out to be a behavioural one.

Our team of five was asked to design a digital service to help Drip, a fast fashion retailer, enter the circular economy without scrapping its existing business model. That sounds like a product problem. It turned out to be more of a behavioural one.

The gap between what people say they value and what they actually do at checkout is the whole design brief. We spent the first weeks staying in that problem space longer than felt comfortable — and the final design is better because of it.

"54% of Gen Z say sustainability matters but rank it below price, fit, and comfort when they buy. 60% say they buy from brands that match their values. Both things are true at the same time. That contradiction is where the design lives."

The Core Insight

Gen Z is not lying when they say sustainability matters. They just won't sacrifice price or convenience for it. The design brief is to make the sustainable choice the convenient one — not to make people feel guilty for choosing otherwise.

Persona

Meet Mary. The user most apps forget to design for.

Alexandra (eco-conscious) and Karen (values-driven activist) were already in the team's thinking. Mary was my addition — and she's the one whose behaviour we most needed to understand. Alexandra is already persuaded. Mary is the whole problem.

🛍️

Mary — Compulsive Buyer

21 · Cork, Ireland · Student · Income ≤ €30k
"I saw a Black Friday discount and just went for it and I didn't really think twice."
Goals
  • Find the best deals on trending clothing quickly
  • Avoid buying items that won't fit or get worn
  • Feel good about purchases without much deliberation
  • Donate or pass on unwanted clothes with minimal effort
Frustrations
  • Regrets impulse buys within days
  • Sustainability features feel preachy
  • No easy way to verify genuine sustainability claims
  • Items frequently don't fit as expected online
Design Implications
  • Discounts are the #1 motivator — surface deals prominently
  • Frame sustainability as cost-saving, not ethical obligation
  • One-tap donate or resell flow post-purchase
  • Build trust through transparent, verifiable data

Designing for Mary meant framing sustainability as cost-saving — not as ethical obligation. That reframe came from actually sitting with her story rather than designing for Alexandra. Most sustainable fashion apps are designed for people who are already persuaded.

Empathy Mapping

What Alexandra's map taught us about affordability.

Empathy Maps were the most useful tool in the project — and the one I least expected to be. Alexandra's map revealed something that completely flipped our affordability conversation.

Says
  • Does this brand use organic or recycled materials?
  • I'd rather buy second-hand than fast fashion
  • I try to donate or recycle my old clothes
  • I'm willing to pay more if it's better for the planet
Thinks
  • Seriously considers sustainability when buying
  • Frustrated by greenwashing in fashion markets
  • Wants to lower personal fashion footprint
  • Trying to make ethical choices on a limited budget
Does
  • Regularly shops second-hand
  • Follows sustainable fashion influencers
  • Reads labels to check eco-credentials
  • Uses apps to track sustainable fashion choices
Feels
  • Proud when making sustainable purchases
  • Frustrated by the high prices of sustainable fashion
  • Skeptical about vague eco-claims and greenwashing
  • Satisfied when extending the life of clothes

The Finding That Changed Everything

Alexandra was not frustrated with Zara. She was frustrated with Patagonia — sustainable alternatives priced out of her student budget. That flipped our whole conversation: we stopped treating low prices as a constraint on sustainability and started treating them as the point.

Alexandra empathy map — the finding that reframed our entire affordability thinking

Key Feature

Verifiable transparency beats brand claims every time.

During ideation I pushed for what became the QR-based Digital Passport. The idea came from two places: Karen's greenwashing skepticism surfaced in empathy mapping, and the EU's Ecodesign for Sustainable Products Regulation — which will require Digital Product Passports for textiles sold in the EU.

"If transparency is a brand claim, Karen doesn't believe it. If it's verifiable by scan, she does."

A shopper scans a QR code and gets origin, materials, and an environmental impact score. That is a different thing from a sustainability pledge in an Instagram caption. A compliance cost for slow movers is a competitive edge for whoever gets there first.

QR Scan feature — verifiable supply chain data, not brand claims

Design Process

Lo-fi to hi-fi. With one important rule.

We co-led the Figma prototyping work. The rule that made it work: everyone sketched independently at lo-fi before comparing. Two features that weren't in the lo-fi emerged at mid-fi — wardrobe tracking and a gamified CO2-saved dashboard. Both came from asking what would bring Mary back to the app after her first purchase.

Lo-fi sketches — independent before comparison, creating genuine design forks to evaluate

"Once you can tap through screens in a browser, the whole team talks differently about the service. You stop saying 'the app would show...' and start pointing at specific things on screen."
The App

Features designed to change behaviour, not describe values.

Every feature was designed to serve Mary's actual motivations — not Alexandra's stated values. That distinction shaped every decision about what made the final cut.

📱

QR-Based Digital Passport

Scan any clothing tag to get origin, materials, and an environmental impact score. Verifiable data, not brand claims. Built ahead of EU Ecodesign compliance requirements.

🎯

Eco Points and Store Credit

Return, donate, or resell items to earn Eco Points redeemable as store credit. Sustainability framed as money saved — not moral credit earned. Mary's primary motivation is always cost.

🗣️

Wardrobe Tracker

Scan existing clothes to build a digital inventory with a per-item sustainability score. Designed to bring users back between purchases — building the habit before changing the behaviour.

⏸️

Constructive Friction

A sustainability score visible before purchase, plus a short cooling-off pause on impulse buys. Intentional friction — not bad UX. The opposite of a dark pattern is not neutral design, it's intentional design.

🏆

Gamified CO2 Dashboard

Visual progress tracker showing CO2 saved, clothes diverted from landfill, and next reward milestone. Makes abstract environmental impact feel immediate and personal for Mary.

🔁

One-Tap Resell and Donate

Post-purchase flow for unwanted items — frictionless resell or donation with minimal steps. Designed for Mary who already donates some clothes but specifically bins fast fashion.

Hi-fi prototype — tapping through screens changed how the team talked about the service

Tool Evaluation

What worked and what needed rethinking.

Not every design thinking tool was equally useful. Being honest about what worked and why is more valuable than listing everything used.

✓ Worked Well

Empathy Maps

Most useful tool in the project. Alexandra's map revealed she was frustrated with Patagonia, not Zara — flipping our entire affordability conversation.

Iterative Prototyping

Independent lo-fi sketching before group comparison prevented committing to the first idea said out loud and refining it forever.

Storyboarding

Made the emotional experience feel real. Following Alexandra across 8 panels forced us to ask what she felt at each step — easiest artefact to explain to someone outside the team.

↻ Needed Rethinking

Stakeholder Map

Went too broad too quickly. Cattle farmers ended up on the board. Lesson: draw the service boundary first, then map inward. We wasted two hours pulling scope back.

Customer Journey Map

Our five-stage map stayed too high-level. Told us roughly where problems were but not what they looked like. Detailed maps are where the design decisions live.

Survey Analysis

26 questions, no pre-agreed analysis plan. Nearly missed a key pattern about younger respondents. Write the analysis framework before data collection — not after.

Customer journey map — five-stage overview (stayed too high-level in hindsight)

Reflection

What I would do differently.

Define the Analysis Framework Before Sending the Survey

We ran a 26-question survey with no pre-agreed analysis plan. When results came back the synthesis was chaotic. Never send a survey without a coding framework already written.

Detail the Repair Booking Flow Specifically

The customer journey map stayed too high-level. Detailing the repair booking sub-flow would have surfaced specific friction points — like the anxiety of posting a garment to a stranger — which would have shaped the feature design.

Draw the Service Boundary Before Mapping Stakeholders

Cattle farmers ended up on the stakeholder map. They're part of the fashion system but completely irrelevant to the digital service. Next time: define scope first, then map inward from there.

Test the Refundable Deposit Model with Users

My deposit model idea was rejected in team discussion as feeling punitive. That might be right — but it should have been tested with a low-fidelity prototype before discarding. Good ideas die in conference rooms all the time for the wrong reasons.

Key Learnings

What I'm taking from this project.

Stay in the Problem Space

My instinct is to arrive at a positioning statement fast and start executing. This project required the opposite. The Mary persona, the value-action gap insight, the decision to lead with economic incentives — none of those would have happened if we moved to solution mode in week two.

Constructive Friction Is Ethical UX

E-commerce UX is built almost entirely around removing friction from the path to checkout. That keeps users like Mary in regret cycles. The designed pauses in the Drip app are not bad UX. The opposite of a dark pattern is not neutral design — it's intentional design.

Design Thinking Transfers Directly to Marketing

The empathy map I built for Alexandra trained me to look for value-action gaps — and those exist in SaaS buyers just as much as in fast fashion shoppers. Different context, same mechanism. Having a proper framework makes the process more deliberate and easier to explain to a team.

Visual Language Lowers Participation Barriers

Our team had different academic backgrounds and varying English fluency. The tools that worked best didn't require you to be articulate in a second language. Visual artefacts democratise collaboration in a way that verbal-only approaches do not.
